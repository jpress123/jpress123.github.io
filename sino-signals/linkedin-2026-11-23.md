Can a celebrity’s permission survive every handoff in an AI content supply chain?

China’s internet regulator reported on November 6 that platforms removed more than 47,000 items and acted against 3,961 accounts associated with AI impersonation of public figures. Its examples include fabricated first-person stories, synthetic voices and manipulated images used to attract followers or promote products.

For China’s creative businesses, the enforcement connects likeness rights to everyday platform operations. A production team’s consent record matters only if distributors and promotional partners can find it when a complaint arrives. The figures describe enforcement activity, with no measure of how much impersonation remains.

Thinking futureback about this signal, what would it take for Western media leaders to make consent verifiable across every distributor and agency? Which contracts and reporting processes must change? If creators cannot establish where permission ends, who controls their digital identity, and who gets excluded from the commercial value it generates?
