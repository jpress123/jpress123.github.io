# Sino-Signals | November 7, 2026 — What a surgeon can feel and an animator can miss

Two Chinese industry reports show where practical expertise enters a technology purchase or a production brief.

A surgeon at a console and an artist at a storyboard face a similar problem: a screen can look convincing while leaving out something a practitioner would notice. This week’s reports offer two ways of catching the discrepancy. One adds a physical cue at the point of action. The other sends the people making the images out to study the action before they draw it.

## Intuitive Fosun Brings Force Feedback to Its China Surgical Showcase

At the China International Import Expo in Shanghai, Intuitive Fosun presented the da Vinci 5 with force feedback. Guangzhou Daily’s November 5 account describes it as the model’s first exhibition in China. The operator remains central: force feedback is information a surgeon can use, not a decision to operate. The company also launched a local digital platform, My Intuitive.

Its reported network of 28 da Vinci training centres matters for a practical reason. A hospital can buy a capability in one transaction; teaching teams what to do with it takes repeated practice. The size of the network is not a count of clinicians proficient on the new model. Nor does the exhibition establish a change in patient outcomes.

For a Western hospital comparing systems, a revealing question would be what the educator can change in a simulation. Can the instructor give two trainees the same scenario, see where their judgments diverge and replay the moment they decided to stop? A procurement specification that asks only whether the equipment provides feedback leaves that teaching decision unanswered.

Monday morning: ask the training lead and a surgeon to write one scenario in which touch and sight give different impressions. Agree on the evidence they would want from a simulator session before deciding how much weight to give the feature in a purchase.

## Drama Makers Learn Papermaking Before Generating Their Storyboards

The makers of 桃花潭记 faced a different problem. They wanted to depict Xuan papermaking, a craft with a long sequence of operations. In a November 4 interview reported by Wenyi Bao, the director described learning from practitioners in Jing County, Anhui. The production used AI for initial storyboards, imagery and music; human creators handled the script, aesthetic choices and review.

That field visit changes the brief. Instead of asking whether a sequence looks like a traditional workshop, the team has a more demanding question available: would carrying out these actions in this order actually produce paper? Other studios can adopt the question. They cannot borrow the answer from a different craft.

For producers elsewhere, the budget line to watch is reference work. If all the money goes into generating and polishing pictures, the person best equipped to spot an error may never see the sequence. A practical contract question follows: is there still time and money to change a shot after a practitioner has seen it?

Monday morning: take one storyboard to someone who performs the process it depicts. Ask which movement they would change first and why. Keep the annotated frame alongside the revision so the next artist can learn from the correction.

One team needs to know when to stop an instrument; the other needs to know when to send a frame back. In both cases, expertise matters most while someone still has the authority and time to act on it.
