What happens when the fastest-growing source of electricity and water demand on earth gets moved to the bottom of the sea?

Shanghai Hailanyun, known as HiCloud, has put the world's first offshore wind powered underwater data center into commercial operation off Shanghai's Lin-gang zone. The roughly 228 million dollar site holds about 2,000 AI servers at 24 megawatts and pulls close to 97 percent of its power from an adjacent offshore wind farm. Seawater cools the sealed modules, cutting cooling electricity by at least 30 percent, using no fresh water, and shrinking land use by about 90 percent. HiCloud has already signed on to build a 500 megawatt version.

Why it matters in China: the country is adding AI compute faster than its grids and water systems can absorb. A design that takes land and fresh water off the table fits a national constraint, and HiCloud went from a Hainan pilot in 2022 to commercial operation in under 30 months, with state agencies siting the facility in a coastal zone by decision. Offshore wind sits right next to the coastal cities generating the demand.

For Western leaders: Microsoft proved this concept with Project Natick off Scotland in 2018, then shelved it. China is now first to commercial scale and building bigger. As US and European operators push data centers into deserts and strain local grids, undersea siting resets the water, land, and carbon math for the whole category. South Korea, Japan, and Singapore are already studying it.

Monday morning: ask your data-center partners for power usage effectiveness, water draw, and siting data, then benchmark against a sub 1.15 ocean-cooled facility. Treat water and land, not only power, as first-order limits on your next AI build.
