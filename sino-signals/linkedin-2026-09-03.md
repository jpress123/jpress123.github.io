How much is five days of warning worth, and who owns the model that gives it to you?

As Typhoon Dolphin closed on the Chinese coast last month, Shanghai AI Laboratory's Fengwu model named the time and place of landfall five days out, within 30 minutes and 30 kilometers. Dolphin then flooded roads and village houses in Yantou town, Wenzhou, on August 10, 2026. Fengwu ran alongside conventional forecasts together with Huawei's Pangu and Fudan University's Fuxi. Its developers report it beat Google's GraphCast on roughly 80 percent of evaluated weather variables and pushed skillful global medium-range forecasting past ten days.

Why this matters in China: three serious AI weather models now come from a state lab, a national champion, and a university, all trained on decades of observation from one of the densest monitoring networks on earth. Typhoon season is a live proving ground every summer. Once trained, these models produce a forecast at a fraction of the compute a numerical supercomputer needs, which puts provincial bureaus and private operators inside the price envelope.

For Western leaders: this puts a Chinese state laboratory in open competition with Google's GraphCast and GenCast, Nvidia-backed FourCastNet, and the ECMWF's AIFS. Catastrophe reinsurers, port operators, airlines, and agricultural traders price exposure on forecast skill, so five-day track accuracy feeds straight into premiums, routing, and hedging. Techwind CTO Sun Zhi is candid about the limits. AI models still trail conventional systems on storm intensity, and they remain untested on seasonal signals like El Nino, which is where the real commercial value sits.

Monday morning: ask your risk and operations teams which weather models actually feed your supply chain, insurance, and continuity decisions, and whether any of them are machine-learned. Run one AI model against your incumbent on last season's storms and compare track error, lead time, and compute cost. Forecast skill is a procurement decision now, with a price attached.
