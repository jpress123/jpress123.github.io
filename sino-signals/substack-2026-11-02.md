## Wangfu Hospital Adds AI Answers to Pharmacy Services

### What happened

Beijing's Wangfu Hospital announced an AI pharmacy question service on October 30. Its account describes a QR-accessible interface combining a language model with pharmacy knowledge. Reference answers are available around the clock, while complex individual medication questions are directed to professional pharmacists. This is the hospital's description of its service, not an independent assessment of accuracy or outcomes.

### Why China can scale this

Our interpretation is that an existing hospital service provides an organisational home for testing such tools. Pharmacy leadership can define content ownership, review responsibilities and boundaries before extending access. That is a possible operating model, not evidence that this deployment has already improved service or spread to other hospitals.

### Global implications

The management question travels well: what should an automated answer resolve, and what must remain a professional conversation? A convincing interface can make the distinction harder for users to see. Leaders should therefore judge a proposed service by its accountability and handoff arrangements, alongside its convenience. The announcement does not quantify workload changes, medication errors or patient benefit.

### Monday morning action

Start with a narrow, professionally reviewed information task. Measure incorrect answers, unsuccessful escalation and whether different patient groups can reach a person without difficulty.

## Licheng Studio Brings Local Heritage Into AI Drama

### What happened

A Licheng studio's AI-assisted cultural short drama premiered on Youku on October 30, according to Shanxi Daily's October 31 report. The creators connect generated imagery with a fantasy narrative rooted in local heritage. The case concerns an actual reported release, rather than a proposal for a future production. It provides no audience or financial results.

### Why China can scale this

Our interpretation is that the combination of local knowledge and an established distribution outlet is more instructive than the choice of generation software alone. Other teams would still need editorial skills, access to suitable material and a route to viewers. Repeatability cannot be inferred from a single release, and distribution should not be confused with demand.

### Global implications

Cultural organisations elsewhere can use this example to examine their own commissioning practices. If an AI-assisted scene misrepresents a place, the important question is who has authority to correct it and whose judgement counts. Creative leadership therefore includes selecting collaborators, documenting permissions and deciding how to assess the audience's understanding. The source does not establish cheaper production or greater visitor spending.

### Monday morning action

Commission a small scene with local storytellers and an accountable editor. Review cultural accuracy and permissions, then test audience understanding and revision effort before ordering a series.

Source: [Shanxi Daily via People's Daily Online](http://sx.people.com.cn/n2/2026/1031/c189130-41737954.html)

These cases suggest two different evaluation questions: can a service hand responsibility back to a professional, and can a production preserve the knowledge that makes its story distinctive? Neither requires assuming that automation alone delivers value. Both invite a small, observable trial with named human owners.
