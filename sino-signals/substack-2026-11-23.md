# Sino-Signals | November 23, 2026 — Who controls the AI transaction?

Two Chinese developments put permission and product clarity at the centre of AI distribution.

Today’s selection covers entertainment and consumer products. One concerns unauthorised digital likenesses, while the other concerns the sale of AI usage through familiar retail channels. The common management question is how a business makes the terms of an AI transaction understandable to everyone who depends on it.

## China Targets AI Impersonation Across Celebrity Content Accounts

In a November 6 enforcement notice, China’s internet regulator reported removing more than 47,000 items and taking action against 3,961 accounts linked to AI impersonation of public figures. Cases included fabricated first-person stories, synthetic voices and manipulated images. The examples distinguish several forms of misuse, including invented personal accounts and material designed to draw audiences into commercial promotion. This is a November 6 notice newly added to the archive today.

The notice names accounts on multiple major platforms. Enforcement addresses the distribution and monetisation of impersonations as well as the generated material. A shared regulator can direct attention across services, although the notice supplies no comparison of detection quality or appeal outcomes between them. A count of removed items therefore needs careful interpretation.

For talent managers and media companies, consent records must travel with digital likenesses. The notice reports enforcement activity rather than measuring how much impersonation remains online. A useful question for a rights holder is whether a distributor can distinguish licensed performance, permitted editing and an unauthorised imitation. Those distinctions should be available to people handling complaints as well as to the original production team.

Trace one licensed digital likeness through production, distribution and promotion. Check who can request removal when an unauthorised version appears. Record the evidence that would support that request and the person responsible for keeping it current. A contract that remains in an agency’s inbox offers limited help to a platform reviewer confronting a disputed clip.

## China’s Shopping Platforms Add Dedicated AI Token Sections

At its November 19 briefing, the Ministry of Commerce said major e-commerce platforms had introduced dedicated token sections during Singles’ Day. It presented purchasing AI computing access as part of the shopping experience. The announcement describes a distribution change, rather than a new model capability. It places AI usage alongside other goods and services consumers encounter during a retail promotion.

Retail platforms already bring buyers, payments and product discovery together. Adding model usage makes an unfamiliar digital service available through an established purchasing channel. The operational challenge is to preserve that familiar purchase experience when the buyer receives an allowance of digital usage instead of a physical item. A receipt alone may tell the buyer little about what a particular task will consume.

The briefing gives no token sales volumes, named vendors or retention data. Retailers elsewhere can examine whether their product descriptions make usage allowances and expiry understandable. Buyers may need to compare different models, renewal arrangements and support channels. Those comparisons become harder when the storefront and the service provider are different organisations.

Walk through a first-time buyer’s purchase and first use. Check how the seller explains model access, usage limits, refunds and recurring charges. Ask customer support to explain what happens when the allowance runs out halfway through a task. Then compare that explanation with what the buyer saw before paying.

[Ministry of Commerce of China](https://www.mofcom.gov.cn/xwfbzt/2026/swbzklxxwfbh2026n11y19r/index.html)

Both stories suggest a practical leadership exercise. Follow a disputed transaction through the organisations involved and identify which evidence each participant can actually see. For a creator, that evidence concerns permission. For a shopper, it concerns what was bought and whether the service delivered it. Clear terms become more valuable as AI moves into channels people already use and trust.
