Sino-Signals | November 9, 2026 — Who gets to vouch for a recommendation?

A shopping platform puts a customer review in front of a buyer. A bank puts a potential supplier across the table from an importer. Two reports from China show how much depends on what people can find out about the recommendation once it reaches them.

## Who actually tried the product?

Guangzhou’s Nansha district issued administrative guidance for sellers and platforms taking part in the November 11 shopping festival. It asks platforms to oversee AI-generated marketing materials, customer-service scripts and virtual livestream hosts. It also asks for prominent labelling of generated marketing imagery. A separate instruction deals with the substance of a review. A seller using generated material must not make up a customer’s experience.

Consider two claims about a rain jacket. An illustration shows water beading on the sleeve. A testimonial says the wearer stayed dry cycling to work. Labelling the illustration helps a shopper understand how the picture was made. Putting the same label on the testimonial still leaves the shopper wondering whether anyone wore that jacket in the rain.

A platform can build the distinction into the process a seller already uses to submit promotional material. The district also asks platforms to keep records of their promotional traffic rules. Those records could help reviewers establish how widely a disputed claim circulated. The guidance sets expectations for this shopping event. The notice supplies no measurements of compliance or changes in shopper behaviour.

For a retail team elsewhere, choose a review that features prominently in a current campaign. Ask the person responsible for the listing to show the evidence behind its strongest claim. Follow the process through to a correction if that evidence cannot be located. [Read the district’s guidance](https://www.gzns.gov.cn/gznsscjg/gkmlpt/content/11/11081/post_11081332.html)

## Which supplier gets a meeting?

Bank of China’s Ningbo branch helped local firms meet prospective partners at the Shanghai import expo. China Financial Information Network describes companies from sectors including medical equipment and international supply chains. Participants used system-assisted matching and then met buyers in person over several rounds. The report describes the bank’s wider history of arranging introductions, but does not establish how many contracts this year’s matching system produced.

The arrangement combines two resources a bank can bring to trade finance. Its platform can surface companies outside a buyer’s immediate network. Its branch staff can bring people together and stay involved after the meeting. An exporter who appears on a shortlist gains the chance to explain a capability that was difficult to express in a company profile.

That makes the missing names worth examining. Ask a relationship manager and a buyer to review one shortlist alongside the original request. Which supplier would the manager add? Does the profile omit a certification, a language capability or experience with small orders? Follow up after the event to learn which introductions advanced and which were unsuitable. [Read the Ningbo report](https://www.cnfin.com/cmjj-lb/detail/20261107/4492436_1.html)

The consumer can ask who tested the jacket. The exporter might never know that a buyer was looking. For the teams designing either service, responsibility starts with deciding who gets to question the recommendation and what that person can do with the answer.
