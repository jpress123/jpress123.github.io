# Sino-Signals | November 26, 2026 — Design the handoff before expanding automation

Vehicle energy recovery and hospital follow-up reveal why integration needs clear responsibilities.

Today’s two entries concern moments when people remain directly involved in an automated process. A driver releases the accelerator; a family reports a change in symptoms. In both cases, the useful leadership question is what happens between the software’s interpretation and the next human decision.

## XPeng Uses AI to Adapt Energy Recovery During Manual Driving

XPeng says its VLA adaptive deceleration feature adjusts energy recovery when a driver releases the accelerator. November 25 event reporting places the first software rollout in December for G9L and MONA L05. This is an announced rollout, not evidence of broad customer adoption. The energy-saving claim must be read alongside the comparison used to produce it.

The company plans to extend the feature to more vehicles through software updates. It integrates road-scene interpretation with vehicle control during manual driving. Distribution through updates gives vehicle makers a practical way to change the experience of an existing product. It also makes release management part of the product’s energy and safety story. Teams need to know which software version a driver experienced when reporting a problem.

XPeng claims 12.4% greater usable range in congested urban conditions compared with energy recovery switched off. That is not an independently verified gain over conventional regenerative braking. For Western vehicle leaders, the broader question is how to make an adaptive response understandable. A driver may value smoothness while still needing confidence that the car will behave predictably. Marketing a percentage improvement should not obscure the operating conditions or the comparison mode.

Ask vehicle teams to compare efficiency, predictability and driver override behaviour against existing regeneration modes. Keep the test baseline visible. Include ordinary journeys and uncomfortable edge cases, and record when a driver intervenes. Ask whether the interface explains which behaviour has changed after an update. A feature that feels effortless still needs a clear account of its boundaries.

## iFlyHealth Connects AI Follow-Up With Hospital Escalation

People’s Daily reported on November 25 that iFlyHealth combines patient records and specialist care pathways in a hospital-linked follow-up service. AI supports questions, symptom recording and alerts for clinical attention. The account illustrates an existing service rather than announcing a new clinical trial. Its reported outcome cannot establish effectiveness across a larger population.

The reported case connects a family using a mobile mini-program with care managers and a doctor. Staff checked worsening symptoms before the doctor recommended further hospital treatment. That arrangement matters because the service connects a patient-facing interface to people who can take action. The value of another communication channel depends on whether somebody is responsible for monitoring and responding to it. More messages can otherwise create more work without improving continuity.

The article describes one patient experience and company statements, not a controlled clinical evaluation. Its practical lesson is to examine the handoff from an automated alert to a named professional. Hospital leaders elsewhere can examine the same boundary in their own services. A system that identifies a concern needs a response process that survives shift changes, staff absences and incomplete patient information. Those requirements should shape procurement as well as implementation.

Trace one high-risk alert from patient submission to clinical response. Check ownership, response times and support for patients who cannot use the app. Ask staff to demonstrate the escalation route using a hypothetical case rather than a sales presentation. Include a family member helping with communication and a patient without reliable smartphone access. Review whether the service makes it clear when online communication is insufficient and another route is required.

[People’s Daily](https://paper.people.com.cn/rmrb/pc/content/202611/25/content_30192978.html)

The common issue is the design of a handoff. In vehicles, a software response must fit human expectations; in follow-up care, an alert must reach someone with authority to respond. Before expanding automation, leaders can make those responsibilities explicit and test whether users and staff understand them.
