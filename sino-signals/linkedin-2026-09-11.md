What happens to your AI vendor strategy when a Chinese cloud provider decides interoperability is worth more than lock-in?

Alibaba Cloud released a new version of QoderWake on September 8. A user describes a role in one line and gets a working AI colleague. Those agents now run inside all three of China's major workplace platforms: Alibaba's own DingTalk, ByteDance's Feishu, and Tencent's WeCom. They pull context on their own from shared documents, calendars, and group chats. Ten roles ship pre-tuned, including product manager, data analyst, UI designer, and front-end and back-end developer. Since April, Alibaba says it has deployed close to 100,000 digital employees that executed roughly 2 million tasks in three months.

Why this matters in China: DingTalk, Feishu, and WeCom together reach most of the country's white-collar workforce. An agent that crosses all three inherits a distribution base no Western equivalent can match. Alibaba is abandoning the walled-garden habit at the application layer because the revenue now sits in workflow execution, not seat licenses. Chinese enterprises also move from pilot to production in weeks, not quarters.

Global impact: Microsoft, Google, and Salesforce each sell agents that work best inside their own suite, and each treats that lock-in as the moat. Alibaba has priced interoperability as the feature instead, which resets what a buyer will accept. Expect procurement teams in Europe and Southeast Asia to start asking whether an agent runs across Teams, Slack, and Google Workspace, and to read a single-suite answer as a limitation.

Monday morning: Audit which of your AI agent contracts assume a single collaboration suite, and price what a cross-platform requirement costs at renewal. Then hand the list of 10 pre-tuned roles to HR. It is a public forecast of which knowledge-work tasks get automated first, and you need an answer on redeployment before your board asks for one.
