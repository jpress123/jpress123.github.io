What does it cost you to build AI capacity in a country that plans its grid and its compute on the same five-year clock?

On August 3, the National Development and Reform Commission and the National Energy Administration released the 15th Five-Year Plan for New Power System Construction, document 942, signed July 21. By 2030 non-fossil sources must supply 50 percent of China's generation. The grid must absorb more than 2.8 billion kilowatts of renewables at high utilization. The charging network must support over 110 million electric vehicles. Conventional hydropower reaches about 410 million kilowatts, nuclear about 110 million. Among the named priority tasks: coordinated development of electricity and computing power.

Why it scales in China: one document binds every provincial energy bureau, both grid operators, and the state generators to the same numeric targets, so planning, capital, and procurement move on one schedule. The plan reaches down to the meter, requiring faster grid-connection service for residential charging points and pilots for unified construction and operation of charging facilities in housing estates. It also names transport and energy convergence as a work item, siting wind, solar, storage, charging and battery-swap stations along expressways and sharing corridors, ducts and pylons between the two networks.

Why Western leaders should care: China is treating data-center demand as a planning input to the power system instead of a shock to it. That is the opposite of how most Western grids are absorbing AI load today. If the compute and electricity build-outs stay coupled, Chinese AI capacity carries a lower and more predictable marginal power cost than capacity built where interconnection queues run five to seven years. The EV charging target sets the reference point European and North American charging economics get measured against for the rest of the decade.

Monday morning: ask your infrastructure team what the interconnection wait is for your next compute site and what delivered power will cost there in 2030. Treat any answer wider than a range as an unpriced risk. Then bring energy procurement into the compute roadmap conversation, this quarter, not next year.
