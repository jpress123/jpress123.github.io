What happens to your AI content policy when the world's largest producer of AI video makes disclosure a condition of distribution?

China's National Radio and Television Administration issued the Administrative Measures for the Development of Micro-Dramas on July 31, and they took effect September 1. Every serialized episode under twenty minutes now falls under one national framework. AI-generated micro-dramas must carry a prominent disclosure label in every episode. Platforms are barred from running recommendation algorithms tuned to compulsive viewing, and they must pre-screen AI output for repeated synthetic faces, infringing material, and visual defects before formal review. Responsibility is assigned across production, review, and distribution.

Why this matters in China: the rules land on a format China already industrialized. In the first half of 2026 the country released 367,000 micro-dramas, more than 74 percent of them AI-generated, at an average of over 1,500 new AI titles a day. Generative tools cut the cost of a title from hundreds of thousands of yuan to a few thousand, so one creator now does work that took a dozen people and several months. Regulating at that volume means regulating the largest working sample of AI video anywhere.

Global impact: provenance metadata becomes an export requirement rather than a voluntary pledge. The same production stack is already traveling. TikTok short dramas paid out more than 110 million dollars in revenue sharing from January to July 2026, with July alone above 29 million. Western platforms treating AI disclosure as a future compliance question will meet Chinese-origin content that already carries it.

Monday morning: ask whether your systems can read, preserve, and display an AI provenance label that arrives attached to third-party video. Then ask whether your own recommendation logic would survive a ban on optimizing for compulsive consumption, because that constraint is now live in a working market.
