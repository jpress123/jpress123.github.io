What happens when a carmaker starts competing for a place in your living room?

Chery subsidiary Mojia Robotics announced the launch of Mobo and opened global reservations in Wuhu on October 21. The product is positioned as a household companion robot.

The evidence is a company launch announcement. Reservations are not deliveries, and a demonstration does not establish reliable performance in an ordinary home.

Why does this matter in China? My reading is that an automotive parent offers a potential route to manufacturing, distribution and service capabilities. The commercial test is whether those capabilities transfer economically to a very different product.

A household is an unforgiving operating environment. Furniture moves, instructions change and different family members expect different things. A useful robot must handle that variation without making its owner a full-time supervisor.

For Western consumer-product leaders, the competitive question extends beyond the machine. Who installs it, maintains it, repairs it and supports it after the initial excitement fades?

A compelling first interaction can win attention. Repeat use, manageable support costs and dependable behaviour are what would justify a lasting place in the home.

I would evaluate the service proposition alongside the hardware. Ask what the product is explicitly designed to do, where human intervention is expected, and how customers recover when a task fails.

Monday morning action: define a small, supervised household trial before making a broader commitment. Record successful tasks, human interventions, support time and voluntary repeat use. Require clear delivery terms, data controls and an easy stop procedure.

Keep the acceptance criteria fixed throughout the trial. Otherwise, it is too easy to turn every failure into a new demonstration and lose sight of the original household need.

The milestone to watch is sustained usefulness after delivery. A reservation announcement starts that test; it does not finish it.
