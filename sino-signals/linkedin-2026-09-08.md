What happens to your product roadmap when a capable agent model costs 22 cents per million input tokens?

Yesterday iFlytek released Spark X2.5, a 293-billion-parameter mixture-of-experts model that activates roughly 30 billion parameters per inference, holds a 256K context window, and covers more than 200 languages. The company says it was trained end to end on a fully domestic compute platform. It went live the same day on iFlytek's Xingchen MaaS platform at 1.6 yuan per million input tokens, 0.24 yuan for cached input, and 6 yuan per million output tokens. A week earlier iFlytek open-sourced two on-device siblings, X2.5-4B and X2.5-1.7B, each with a one-million-token context window and each trained on the same domestic stack. The upgrade targets code generation and agent orchestration.

Why this scales in China: the price is the distribution strategy. An agent workflow that fires thousands of calls to finish one task becomes affordable to mid-sized manufacturers and school districts that would never license a frontier Western model. iFlytek already sells into tens of thousands of Chinese classrooms and sits on government procurement lists, and domestic-silicon provenance clears the procurement rules that now favor local compute. The open-weight 4B and 1.7B versions push the same capability onto learning tablets and factory edge boxes with no cloud bill.

Why Western leaders should care: training a 293B model on Chinese accelerators moves domestic silicon past the inference-only assumption that held through 2025, alongside Meituan's trillion-parameter run on 50,000 domestic chips and Baidu's Kunlunxin training of Ernie 5.1. Export controls shaped which chips China buys. They have done less to shape what China can train.

Monday morning: have your engineering lead price one real agent workflow, the kind that makes 5,000 model calls per task, against Spark X2.5 rates and against your current vendor. An order-of-magnitude gap is a cost problem before it is a capability problem.
